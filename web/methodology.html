Methodology

Why these five checks

Every research note we produce carries a five-point Conviction Checklist. This is the reasoning behind which five, why those thresholds, and what we removed along the way — with the measurements that decided it.

Policy version 2.2 Measured on 50 large-cap US equities 1 September 2026

What it is, and what it is not

The checklist is supplementary evidence, not a rating. It never renames, replaces or overrides the seven-label rating a note carries, and it is not an input to it. It is a second, transparent view of the same evidence, so a reader can see at a glance how many independent conditions the current picture actually satisfies.

Every criterion is deterministic and threshold-based. No model judgment and no generated prose is an input. Given the same data on the same day, it returns the same five answers — which is the point: it is the part of a note you can check.

The bar a criterion has to clear

A checklist is only worth reading if each box can come out either way, and if the five are not quietly measuring the same thing. We hold candidates to four tests before they earn a place.

01  Discriminates

It must pass for some names and fail for others. A box ticked for nine names in ten cannot separate them, however sound it sounds.

02  Independent

It must add a lens rather than restate one. Five correlated checks are one check reported five times, and they let a single strong theme sweep the score.

03  Computable

It must be evaluable for the securities we actually cover. A criterion that is routinely blank is worse than no criterion at all.

04  Deterministic

It must be a stated threshold on a stated figure, so the reader can disagree with the rule rather than with a judgment they cannot see.

The five

Three read price from different angles; two read the business. Measured pass rates are in the right-hand column — each sits in the range where a box actually separates names.

Check Confirms when Passes
Trend Price is above both its 50-day and 200-day averages. Two horizons, so a single sharp week cannot carry it. 46%
Momentum MACD is above its signal and RSI sits between 40 and 75 — trending with participation, short of the overbought extreme where a pullback becomes likely. 32%
Relative strength Total return beats the S&P 500 over the same dates by at least three percentage points. Outperformance, not simply a rising price in a rising market — and not a dead heat either. 48%†
Quality Return on equity above 15%. The business earns a strong return on the capital shareholders have in it — profitable, not merely in the black. 68%
Revisions Next-year consensus earnings are higher than 90 days ago. Not where expectations stand, but which way they are moving. 71%

Quality earned its place on independence as much as merit: measured against every other criterion, its correlation ran between −0.27 and +0.07. It genuinely adds a lens rather than restating the price-based ones.

† Relative strength’s 48% was measured under version 2.1, which required only that the stock beat the index rather than beat it by a margin. The margin will lower that figure; it has not yet been re-measured, and we would rather say so than quote a number we have not taken.

What we removed, and why

Version 1 carried two criteria that sounded right and did no work. We only found that out by measuring them, which is now the rule: nothing joins or leaves the checklist on argument alone.

Removed  ·  Street conviction

The analyst mean price target sits above the current price.

It passed 90% of the time. Median implied upside was +13.7%, and the worst case across fifty large caps was only −4.8%. Sell-side targets carry a documented upward bias, so the box was very nearly always ticked — and a box ticked for nine names in ten cannot separate them.

Worse, it correlated negatively with trend (−0.23) and momentum (−0.34). Because targets lag price, it was quietly rewarding stocks that had fallen — the opposite of what a reader takes from the words “street conviction”.

Removed  ·  Growth

Trailing revenue growth and earnings growth both positive.

It was the least computable criterion we had, evaluable on 86% of names, and it looks backwards. Revisions ask the same question — is this business getting better? — in the direction that actually moves prices. Trailing growth is still reported in the fundamentals section of every note; it is simply no longer one of the five boxes.

What changed in 2.2

Re-thresholded  ·  Relative strength

The criterion had been a bare comparison: beat the index by anything at all. So a stock returning +1.5% against the S&P 500’s +1.4% confirmed it, and the note told the reader it was ahead of the market. Over a three-month window a tenth of a point is noise, and a box that turns on noise cannot separate names however sound the factor behind it.

It now requires a lead of three percentage points. Note that this is a statement about measurement, not a demotion of the factor: relative strength is among the most replicated predictors in the literature, and the fault was in reading a rounding error as outperformance.

One consequence is worth stating plainly. A stock ahead by two points now misses the check while still being ahead, so the wording had to change with the rule: a miss reads “not clearly ahead of the S&P 500”, never “behind”. A threshold that made the report say something untrue would have been a worse fault than the one it fixed.

How we read that

Four of five is a constructive picture, and every price-based lens agrees: the trend is intact, momentum is participating, and the stock has beaten the market comfortably. On the business, returns on capital are exceptional.

The one that does not confirm is the interesting one. Analysts have been cutting next-year earnings while the price rose 42%. That is not a reason to sell, and the note still carried a constructive rating. It is a reason to know precisely what you are relying on: the case rests on price and profitability, and not on expectations improving — because they have not been.

So the note says what to watch: estimates turning back up. That is the whole idea. The checklist is not there to produce a score. It is there to make the shape of a view explicit, and to name the thing that would change it before anyone has to argue about it.

Rules that keep it honest

01

A gap is stated, never guessed. If a criterion cannot be evaluated — a security too new to have a 200-day average, no consensus estimate at any lookback — it is reported as not confirmed with the reason given. It never counts toward or against the score.

02

“Does not apply” is different from “not available”. A fund has no return on equity and no earnings consensus. Saying the data was unavailable would invite someone to retry for something that does not exist, so those two boxes say plainly that they do not apply.

03

Direction, where magnitude is undefined. Revisions judge which way an estimate moved, so a loss-making company whose forecast narrows still confirms it. An earlier rule required a positive base and silently excluded every loss-maker — growth names and turnarounds among them.

04

Changing it is a versioned decision. Adding, removing or re-thresholding a criterion bumps the policy version and is recorded with the measurement that justified it. This document describes version 2.2.

The checklist is supplementary evidence and not a recommendation. Ratings, levels and scenarios in our notes are evidence-based assessments, not guarantees, and investing involves risk including possible loss of principal. Internal use only — not for client distribution.
